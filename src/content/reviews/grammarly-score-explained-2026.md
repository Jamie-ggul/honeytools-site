# Does a High Grammarly Score Actually Mean Good Writing?

**TL;DR:** The Grammarly score is a useful proxy, not a grade. It mostly reflects mechanical correctness — grammar, spelling, punctuation, and some clarity signals — not whether your writing actually lands with the reader. Chasing 100 is fine as a checklist habit, but it's not the same thing as chasing good writing.

## Why Listen to Me on This

I use Grammarly's free version to proofread finished writing — mainly formal reports and emails — as a second check before hitting send, especially since I'm writing in a language that isn't my first. The score is the first thing I see every time I paste text in, and after enough rounds of it, you start to notice what actually moves the number and what doesn't. This post is that pattern-recognition, not a marketing explanation of the feature.

## What the Score Is Actually Built From

Grammarly breaks its score down into a handful of categories — correctness (grammar, spelling, punctuation), clarity, engagement, and delivery (tone-related suggestions). The free plan weighs heavily on the first one; the more style-oriented categories lean on Premium's suggestions. In practice, the number you see is dominated by how many mechanical issues are left unresolved in the document, not by an assessment of whether the argument holds together or the email actually gets the point across.

## What Moves the Number Fastest

The fastest score gains come from fixing exactly the kind of small, mechanical slips that are easy to miss when you're focused on content rather than form — a missing article, an inconsistent verb tense, a misplaced comma. These are also the errors non-native writers tend to make most often, since they're about the "feel" of a language rather than a rule you can consciously check line by line. That's exactly why the score climbs quickly once you accept the first pass of suggestions — you're clearing out the highest-volume, lowest-effort category first.

## What a High Score Doesn't Tell You

A document can score close to 100 and still be a weak piece of writing. The score doesn't know whether your report's structure makes sense, whether the argument you're making is convincing, whether the tone is right for the person reading it, or whether you've buried the actual point three paragraphs in. Those are judgment calls a grammar checker isn't built to make — it's checking the sentences, not the thinking behind them.

## Should Non-Native Writers Trust the Score More?

Somewhat — but not blindly. If your main worry is "does this sound like a native speaker wrote it," the score is a genuinely useful signal, because mechanical fluency (articles, prepositions, agreement) is exactly the category where non-native writing tends to lose points, and exactly the category the score weighs most heavily. What it won't catch is whether your phrasing sounds natural versus just grammatically valid — "technically correct but nobody talks like that" sentences can still score well.

## A More Useful Way to Read It

Treat the score as a floor, not a ceiling. Getting a document into the high 90s is a reasonable bar for "no embarrassing mechanical mistakes are going to distract the reader." It's not a bar for "this is well-written." For anything that matters — a report going to leadership, an email to a client — the score is worth clearing before you send, but it shouldn't be the last thing you check.

## FAQ

**Is getting to 100 worth the effort?**
Usually not on its own. Past the high 90s, you're often accepting suggestions that don't meaningfully change how the writing reads. Diminishing returns set in fast.

**Does the free plan give the same score as Premium?**
The score itself is available on the free plan, but Premium surfaces more suggestions in the clarity/engagement/delivery categories — so a free-plan score can sometimes look artificially high simply because it isn't flagging as much in those areas yet.

**Can a low score still be good writing?**
Yes — a document full of intentional sentence fragments, informal tone, or stylistic choices a grammar checker doesn't recognize can score lower while reading perfectly well for its actual audience. Context matters more than the number.

## Final Thoughts

The Grammarly score is a decent gut-check for mechanical correctness, and it's a genuinely helpful signal if English isn't your first language and you want a fast way to catch the errors that are hardest to self-edit. But it's answering a narrower question than "is this good writing" — and treating it like a grade instead of a checklist is the easiest way to over-trust it.
