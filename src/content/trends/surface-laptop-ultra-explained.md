**Update, October 24, 2026:** The Surface Laptop Ultra shipped on October 16, and independent reviews are now out. We've added a section on what Windows Central, PCWorld, and Gizmodo found, and revised the battery, app-compatibility, and "worth it" answers that previously said no independent data existed. Microsoft's and Nvidia's own claims are still labeled as theirs.

**TL;DR:** The Surface Laptop Ultra is Microsoft's most expensive and most AI-focused laptop yet: a 15-inch Windows on Arm machine built on Nvidia's RTX Spark chip, with up to 128GB of memory shared between CPU and GPU. It started shipping October 16 at $2,599, climbing to $5,899.99. Reviewers describe strong graphics, roughly 10 hours of real-world battery life, and a CPU that trails Apple's top chips, along with Windows on Arm quirks, a heavy chassis, and a price that only makes sense for AI developers and GPU-heavy creators. Windows Central calls it the closest Windows has come to a MacBook Pro but not a perfect alternative yet, and Gizmodo gave it an Editor's Choice badge. None of the reviews we read published measured speeds for large local AI models, so the "120 billion parameters" claim is still mostly Microsoft's.

## A Quick Note on How This Was Put Together

This is a research roundup, not a hands-on review; we haven't used the laptop. The launch specs and prices come from TechRepublic, The Gadgeteer, Gadget Review, and a WindowsForum recap. The October 24 update draws on published reviews from Windows Central, PCWorld, and Gizmodo, plus the first part of a ServeTheHome review (hardware and ports only; we didn't get its benchmark pages). Reviewers tested different configurations, which we note below, so numbers aren't always comparable. Where sources disagreed, we say so. Check Microsoft's store for current configurations before ordering.

## What Independent Reviewers Found

| Area | What reviewers reported |
|---|---|
| Graphics | PCWorld calls GPU performance "very impressive," closer to a high-end gaming laptop than an RTX 5060 model in a ComfyUI test. In Gizmodo's tests, the 3DMark Steel Nomad score was about 37% above an ASUS ProArt PX13, and a 40-core Apple GPU scored about 22% higher than the Surface. |
| CPU | Mixed. Windows Central calls it "okay" and roughly on par with a Snapdragon X2 Elite or last-generation Apple M4 Pro. Gizmodo measured an M5 Max MacBook Pro about 44% faster in Geekbench 7 multi-core and about 11% faster in Cinebench 2026 multi-core. PCWorld calls it "surprisingly average" and cites single-core scores less than half of Apple's. |
| Battery | Windows Central measured about 14 hours in the Procyon test and about 10 hours in real use, roughly halved under heavy GPU work. PCWorld saw about 10 hours in an office-work benchmark. Gizmodo said most of a workday in power-efficiency mode and 2 to 3 hours when gaming or running GPU-heavy AI tasks. |
| Heat and noise | Windows Central recorded the keyboard deck peaking at 123°F with no throttling, fans at about 42 dB, and audible coil whine under GPU load. |
| Display | Mini-LED, 3K, 120Hz. Windows Central found blooming and backlight ghosting and wished for OLED. |
| Compatibility | Many apps run natively, others rely on Microsoft's Prism emulation. PCWorld says Adobe Premiere needed a beta build to recognize the GPU. Gizmodo reported slow load times for some apps and games, plus glitches like flashing screens. |
| Software | Windows Central reported restarts taking over two minutes and a default "Best Power Efficiency" profile that can cut performance substantially; it recommends Balanced on battery and Best Performance when plugged in. Copilot+ features were not available at launch, with Microsoft saying they are coming. |

**Which configuration was tested matters.** Windows Central and Gizmodo reviewed the 128GB model (about $5,900 to $6,000), while PCWorld reviewed a $3,699.99 unit it described as 32GB. The $3,699.99 price was listed at Amazon, B&H, and Best Buy, which supports the mid configuration we had previously flagged as single-source.

**What reviewers said about the physical machine.** It's heavy for a 15-inch laptop at about 4.4 to 4.5 pounds; ServeTheHome measured 19.16mm thick, slightly above the "under 18mm" Microsoft figure and Gizmodo's 18mm. Windows Central praised the keyboard and the large haptic trackpad. It lacks pen support.

**The verdicts.** Windows Central says it's the closest Windows has had to a MacBook Pro but not the perfect alternative yet, recommends it mainly for AI developers and high-end creators, and says the base model doesn't justify its extra cost over a Surface Laptop 8 with 24GB of RAM. Gizmodo says it isn't a direct MacBook Pro rival in raw performance but is a strong laptop worth buying, with an Editor's Choice 2026 badge. PCWorld is more skeptical on cost, noise, and heat when running local AI. PCWorld didn't give a score.

## What It Is

The Surface Laptop Ultra is a 15-inch laptop built around Nvidia's RTX Spark platform, which pairs an Nvidia Grace CPU (18 or 20 cores depending on the model) with a Blackwell RTX GPU (5,120 or 6,144 cores). CPU and GPU share a single pool of "unified" memory, from 24GB up to 128GB. That shared pool is the key to the local-AI pitch: large AI models need a lot of memory, and a laptop that can offer 128GB to one chip can hold models that most laptops can't.

Other headline specs reported across the coverage:

- **Display:** 15-inch mini-LED PixelSense Ultra touchscreen, up to 2,000 nits peak HDR (measured on a small portion of the screen, so sustained brightness is lower)
- **Size:** about 18 to 19mm thick and about 4.4 to 4.5 pounds, depending on whose measurement you use
- **Ports:** three USB-C (including a magnetic charging port), USB-A, HDMI, SD card reader, and a headphone jack
- **Cooling:** two fans, and Microsoft says the thermal system has about 2.5 times the capacity of its current Surface laptops
- **Pen support:** none — the Surface Pen and Slim Pen aren't supported

## The Local AI Claim, Explained

Microsoft says the laptop can run models of up to 120 billion parameters on the device. Two things to understand about that:

- **It's a ceiling, not a guarantee.** One review-style writeup called it "a capability ceiling, not a daily-use guarantee." Whether a 120B model runs *well* depends on quantization (compressing the model), memory bandwidth, and how well the software is optimized. A model that fits in memory can still be slow.
- **The headline compute figure is theoretical.** Microsoft cites up to 1 petaflop of AI compute on some configurations, which is a peak figure at very low numerical precision using a technique called sparsity. It doesn't predict real-world speed.

Microsoft's own comparisons against a MacBook Pro with an M5 Pro chip claim roughly 2.1 times faster time-to-first-token and 4.3 times faster image generation. We didn't find those claims tested in the reviews we read. What reviewers did confirm is narrower: the laptop can run local models, and PCWorld and Gizmodo each ran the Hermes agent on it, with Gizmodo running an Artificial Analysis agent benchmark. Neither published tokens-per-second figures for large models in what we reviewed, and PCWorld said it couldn't tell whether multitasking hurt AI performance. Windows Central noted the 128GB configuration can fit large models on the GPU, and PCWorld flagged fan noise and power draw under AI workloads.

The practical value of local AI is privacy and control: your prompts and files stay on your machine, and you don't pay per-token cloud fees. Microsoft is also pushing a hybrid approach, where some work runs locally and some goes to the cloud. A GitHub tool called HydraFusion that routes coding tasks between local and cloud models was slated for an experimental preview later in October, which ties into the agent strategy we covered in our [Microsoft Copilot Agents explainer](/trends/microsoft-copilot-agents-explained/). For a sense of what hosted models cost compared with buying hardware, our [LLM API cost calculator](/tools/llm-api-cost-calculator/) lets you model your own usage. Another very large local-AI option arrived this month in our [Muse Spark Open explainer](/trends/muse-spark-open-explained/), though that model needs far more memory than any laptop offers.

## The Catches

- **Windows on Arm.** This isn't a traditional x86 Windows PC. Reviewers hit emulation quirks, long load times in some apps, and at least one major app (Premiere) needing a beta. Verify the apps you rely on.
- **Gaming is workable but uneven.** Windows Central said most games run at 1440p high settings above 60fps; Gizmodo saw Gears of War: E-Day at around 40fps on high with DLSS, where a $3,016 Lenovo Legion laptop exceeded 60fps. *Call of Duty* support is slated for 2027.
- **Battery is "fine," not class-leading.** About 10 hours of real-world use is respectable, but it drops sharply under GPU or AI load.
- **Heat, noise, and weight.** Reviewers noted loud fans at full power, coil whine under GPU load, and a chassis heavier than a 14-inch MacBook Pro.
- **Early software rough edges.** Slow restarts, a conservative default power profile, and missing Copilot+ features at launch.
- **No pen support**, which matters for illustrators and note-takers.
- **Price.** At $2,599 to nearly $6,000, reviewers say the premium mainly makes sense for local AI development, engineering, or heavy creative work, not email and browsing.

## Who Should Actually Care About This

**If you run or want to run large AI models locally** (developers, researchers, privacy-sensitive teams), this is one of the first laptops aimed squarely at you, and reviewers say the 128GB configuration is the one built for that. Expect a loud, warm machine when you do.

**If you're a creator with GPU-heavy workloads**, the graphics are the draw, though app support on Arm (Adobe especially) needs checking for your specific tools.

**If you mostly use AI through ChatGPT, Claude, or Copilot in the browser**, you don't need this. A cheaper laptop plus a cloud subscription will serve you better.

## FAQ

**When does the Surface Laptop Ultra come out?**
It began shipping October 16, 2026, after preorders opened October 7.

**How much does it cost?**
It starts at $2,599 and goes up to $5,899.99 for the top configuration with 128GB of memory. The 32GB configuration reviewers tested was $3,699.99.

**Can it run AI models locally?**
Yes. Microsoft says up to 120 billion parameters, which is an upper bound from the company. Reviewers confirmed local models and agents run, but we didn't find published speed measurements for the largest models.

**What's the battery life?**
Windows Central measured about 14 hours in Procyon and about 10 hours in real use, and PCWorld also saw about 10 hours in an office benchmark. Expect far less under gaming or AI workloads: Gizmodo reported 2 to 3 hours.

**Is it worth it over a MacBook Pro?**
It depends. In Gizmodo's tests an M5 Max MacBook Pro was faster on CPU benchmarks and slightly ahead in some GPU tests, while the Surface led some others. Windows Central says it's the closest Windows has come but not a perfect alternative. The Surface's case rests on CUDA and Windows software, not raw speed.

**Does it run regular Windows apps?**
It runs Windows on Arm, so many x86 apps run through emulation, and reviewers hit some compatibility and load-time issues. Check the apps you depend on before buying.

## Final Thoughts

The Surface Laptop Ultra is less a mainstream laptop launch than a signal that local AI is becoming a hardware category of its own. The reviews paint a consistent picture: very good graphics, decent battery, a CPU that's good but not Apple-beating, and a long list of early-platform rough edges. The 128GB model is a specialist tool and the cheaper configurations are harder to justify. If you're tempted, test your specific apps first, and watch for software updates, since several of the biggest complaints (restart times, Copilot+ features, shader loading) are things Microsoft says it is working on.
